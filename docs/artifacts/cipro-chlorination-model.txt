What leaves the plant after chlorine and bisulfite?
Chlorine turns ciprofloxacin into N-chloro-cipro in under a second. That breaks apart over minutes into products that all keep the quinolone core, the part that attacks bacteria. Bisulfite then removes the chlorine and may turn leftover N-chloro-cipro back into cipro. The flow pattern in your tube is predicted from its size and the pump flow. Faster pumping shortens the contact time and also changes how the water moves. In the shaded range, flow is slow enough that diffusion across the tube evens out the speeds (Taylor dispersion), so the tube acts almost like an ideal pipe. At faster flow, water near the centre moves about twice the average speed and leaves early. Coiling narrows that spread, so the real tube sits between the two lines. The November dye test decides where.

Second check: Kennedy Neth et al. 2019 (chlorine used up)
2.33 mg/L cipro + 2.0 mg/L chlorine in ultrapure water, no chlorine demand, pH assumed 7 (not stated in the supplement). Tests how much chlorine cipro and its products consume. Nothing fitted to these numbers; the 2-chlorine step comes from Dodd's product structures.
Kennedy Neth 2019, Fig. 2A: cipro and activity over time (fails)
They stopped the reaction with hydrogen peroxide and measured cipro by LC-MS. About 30% of the cipro stayed for 2 hours. Dodd's kinetics, and so this model, say none should survive. The two labs disagree, and the authors note their rate was about 100× slower than published values. Their antibacterial activity (PEQ) stayed about 0.4 above what the leftover cipro explains, which supports active products. Values are digitized from their figure.

What to try
Gap 2:
drag the reversal speed right with a 10-minute contact time. Short contact leaves more N-chloro-cipro for bisulfite to turn back.
Gap 1:
switch ammonia to "Added with chlorine". Cipro still disappears instantly, because it grabs free chlorine about 10× faster than ammonia does. Only "Pre-formed chloramine" slows it down, so pre-form the chloramine in the experiment.
Mixing:
pick "1 tank". More N-chloro-cipro leaves intact than with an ideal pipe.
Pump flow:
drop the contact time below about 40 minutes in a 20 m × 3.2 mm tube. The flow chart leaves the shaded Taylor range, and the predicted line rises above the ideal pipe.
Dichloramine:
pick "Added with chlorine", 3 mg/L chlorine and 0.5 mg N/L ammonia (6:1). Most of the chlorine ends up as dichloramine instead of free chlorine.
Validation:
switch the breakdown speed to the Jasper-scaled value and watch the error against Dodd's real-water data change.
Every product the model tracks keeps the quinolone ring, so "drug removed" and "antibacterial molecules removed" are different numbers. Kennedy Neth 2019 found that some later products lose the carboxyl group or the fluorine, which should switch off activity, so "keeps the ring" is an upper limit on what stays active. The E. coli test decides.

Gap 2
Updated Oct 15: dichloramine added (it matters only above about 5:1 chlorine to ammonia), and the flow pattern is predicted from tube diameter, length and pump flow (Reynolds number, Taylor dispersion or laminar convection, Dean number for a 100 mm coil). The laminar case treats each streamline as its own beaker. Updated Oct 6: later products take 2 chlorines each (Dodd Scheme 1), and chloramine slowly breaks down. Not modelled yet: chloramine reacting with the water's own organic matter, products that lose activity (Kennedy Neth), temperature (values near 22–25 °C), mixing at the dosing point, and how much coiling narrows the spread. Solver: implicit (backward Euler) steps, checked against the Python version in the GitHub repo to within 0.3%.
