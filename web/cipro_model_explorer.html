What leaves the plant after chlorine and bisulfite?

Chlorine turns ciprofloxacin into N-chloro-cipro in under a second. That breaks apart over minutes into products that all keep the quinolone core, the part that attacks bacteria. Bisulfite then removes the chlorine and may turn leftover N-chloro-cipro back into cipro. Move any knob and every chart recomputes. Constants are from Dodd et al. 2005 unless marked.

Full model vs simpler baselines

Each model predicts Dodd's real-water data. The fitted curve is one exponential decay fitted to the other water, so it is also tested on data it never saw. The full model is never fitted. Lower error wins.

The drug-only baseline says cipro is gone in under a second, so it also predicts nothing left for bisulfite to turn back and no antibacterial molecules at discharge. The fitted curve has no pH, so it can't predict a different pH, product formation or Gap 2. Move the breakdown-speed knobs to see the full model's score change.

What to try

  • Gap 2: drag the reversal speed right with a 10-minute contact time. Short contact leaves more N-chloro-cipro for bisulfite to turn back.
  • Gap 1: switch ammonia to "Added with chlorine". Cipro still disappears instantly, because it grabs free chlorine about 10× faster than ammonia does. Only "Pre-formed chloramine" slows it down, so pre-form the chloramine in the experiment.
  • Mixing: pick "1 tank". More N-chloro-cipro leaves intact than with an ideal pipe.
  • Validation: switch the breakdown speed to the Jasper-scaled value and watch the error against Dodd's real-water data change.
Every product the model tracks keeps the quinolone core, so "drug removed" and "antibacterial molecules removed" are different numbers. Whether the products still stop bacteria is what the E. coli test will measure.
